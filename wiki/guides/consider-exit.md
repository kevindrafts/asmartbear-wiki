# How should I think about an acquisition offer?

**Use this when:** A concrete offer creates a choice between liquidity and continued ownership. This guide organizes reflection, not legal, tax, or valuation advice.

1. **Define what the money changes.** Cohen's sale account focuses on securing a meaningful personal threshold rather than maximizing speculative upside. Your threshold and obligations may differ. [Risk and personal utility](https://longform.asmartbear.com/rich-vs-king-sold-company/).
2. **Compare realistic alternatives.** Continued independence has risks and will change the founder's job as the company grows. Do not compare a sale only with an idealized version of today's work. [The changing role](https://longform.asmartbear.com/identity-selling-sadness/).
3. **Separate stakeholders.** Consider plausible outcomes for employees, customers, and owners independently. An acquisition is neither automatically a failure nor automatically beneficial to everyone. [Different consequences](https://longform.asmartbear.com/acquisition-failure/).
4. **Consider identity and purpose.** List the relationships and activities that would remain after the transaction, and what kind of work would feel worthwhile next. Financial security need not immediately replace the meaning supplied by the business. [Work that fits](https://longform.asmartbear.com/fulfillment/); [Meaning beyond the peak](https://longform.asmartbear.com/success-guaranteed/).
5. **Evaluate the actual deal.** Use qualified advisers to assess the offer's terms and consequences. Keep historical multiples and other founders' anecdotes separate from your own transaction evidence. [Context of the original decision](https://longform.asmartbear.com/rich-vs-king-sold-company/).

**Leave with:** A clear account of desired outcomes, acceptable risks, stakeholder concerns, and questions for diligence. A later good or bad market cannot prove the unchosen future was certain, and no essay predicts your emotional response.
