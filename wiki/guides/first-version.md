# What should version one include?

**Use this when:** The first release keeps expanding, or a working prototype does not yet produce a complete customer outcome.

1. **Write the smallest worthwhile promise.** Specify one user in one situation and the result they can finish. Cut adjacent jobs before cutting essential steps in that result. [A small, complete product](https://longform.asmartbear.com/slc/).
2. **Name the reason to choose it.** Decide which benefit is strong enough for the intended customer to accept the remaining limits. It may be convenience, design, service, or a distinctive capability. [The decisive benefit](https://longform.asmartbear.com/the-important-thing/).
3. **Identify the fatal assumption.** Before investing in polish, test whether the technology can deliver the promise, whether buyers care, and whether you can reach them. Build the test around the uncertainty rather than the part you most enjoy. [Investigate the risky condition](https://longform.asmartbear.com/startup-drake-equation/).
4. **Get evidence from use.** Observe first-time users and ask abandoners what prevented the outcome. Do not infer that every weak conversion result requires another feature. [Learn from lost sales](https://longform.asmartbear.com/put-down-the-compiler/).
5. **Record the obligations created.** Choose acceptable initial limitations explicitly, protect essential safety and reliability, and track shortcuts that future adoption will make expensive. [Plan to repay quality debt](https://longform.asmartbear.com/software-quality-mortgage/).

**Leave with:** A narrow end-to-end release, clear exclusions, an observation plan, and known maintenance obligations. Completeness does not mean feature parity, and a small product can remain small if it serves the intended business. The release remains an experiment rather than proof of fit.
