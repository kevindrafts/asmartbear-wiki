# Capacity, recovery, and the cost of commitment

The corpus contains a real tension. An early essay describes bootstrapping as consuming almost all available attention and accepts substantial sacrifice; later and adjacent essays argue that fragmented attention, inadequate recovery, and chronic overload reduce output. Both perspectives belong in the record. [The 2009 sacrifice argument](https://longform.asmartbear.com/sacrifice-your-health-for-your-startup/); [Focus and recovery](https://longform.asmartbear.com/stop-chopping-to-pieces/).

The utilization analogy explains why spare capacity is operationally useful. When every person is overloaded, ordinary interruptions propagate through the team. The two-big-things essay similarly forces attention to competing commitments, although its fixed numerical limit is a provocation rather than a measured law. [Brittle capacity](https://longform.asmartbear.com/utilization/); [Competing commitments](https://longform.asmartbear.com/two-big-things/).

**Editorial application:** Make a realistic capacity plan with affected people, preserve recovery, and distinguish a short, purposeful surge from the normal operating baseline. If emergencies continually consume strategic work, investigate staffing, dependencies, scope, and promises. Successful founders' anecdotes do not prove that damaging health or relationships causes success, and these essays are not medical guidance.
