# An exit changes both risk and identity

Cohen's explanation of selling Smart Bear centers on the value of securing personal freedom, not maximizing every possible future dollar. Continued ownership offered upside but retained concentrated risk; profitability also made walking away from an offer possible. [The sale decision](https://longform.asmartbear.com/rich-vs-king-sold-company/).

That financial decision does not resolve identity. Selling can remove a meaningful role, but staying does not preserve the original role either: a larger company demands different work. Customers, employees, and founders may experience an acquisition differently, so a blanket success-or-failure verdict is inadequate. [Identity after a sale](https://longform.asmartbear.com/identity-selling-sadness/); [Different stakeholders, different futures](https://longform.asmartbear.com/acquisition-failure/).

**Editorial application:** Compare plausible futures for the business and for your own daily life. Separate the financial threshold you need from status comparisons, and include the risks of remaining independent. Historical deal multiples and anecdotes are not current valuation guidance. No article can establish how a particular acquisition will affect every stakeholder or how a founder will feel afterward.

The [DevriX conversation](../../processed/smart-bear-live-e09.md) separates a buyer seeking an operating agency from one seeking its technical capability. Cohen explicitly acknowledges that the latter buyer is hypothetical; Jon Sturgeon emphasizes reducing founder dependence even without a sale. **Editorial connection:** Specify why a particular buyer would want the business before treating an attractive sale story as an available option. The conversation supplies possible motives, not valuation evidence or an offer.
